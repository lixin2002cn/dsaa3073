01 / 09 · From experts to vectors

Choose a feasible point before seeing its loss

Define the comparator and turn a curved loss difference into a linear one. PDF pp. 2–4

The main idea

On round \(t\), choose \(x_t\) in a fixed feasible set \(\mathcal K\), then observe a convex loss \(f_t\) and incur \(f_t(x_t)\). Compare with one feasible point used throughout the sequence:

\[R_T=\sum_{t=1}^T f_t(x_t)-\min_{u\in\mathcal K}\sum_{t=1}^T f_t(u).\]

At the played point, the observed gradient \(g_t=\nabla f_t(x_t)\) gives the upper comparison

\[f_t(x_t)-f_t(u)\le g_t^\top(x_t-u).\]
Past informationPlay feasible \(x_t\)Reveal \(f_t\)Prepare \(x_{t+1}\)
What is fixed, and what can change?

The source assumes a nonempty compact convex \(\mathcal K\subseteq\mathbb R^d\), and losses differentiable on a neighborhood of that set. Compactness means closed and bounded here. Convexity means every segment between feasible points remains feasible. Intervals, boxes, and Euclidean balls qualify.

The set stays fixed, while \(x_t\) and \(f_t\) change. The function \(f_t\) assigns a value to every feasible point; \(f_t(x_t)\) is one scalar. Full loss-function feedback arrives after play. OGD needs the current gradient; minimizing all past original functions can use the fuller feedback.

Listing a ten-choice grid in ten coordinates would require \(10^{10}\) entries before constraints, while a vector has ten coordinates. Gradients exploit geometry without enumerating all candidate points. Losses may change arbitrarily and can be negative; the coming assumptions bound gradients and geometry rather than losses in \([0,1]\).

Keep one comparator and derive linearization

Continuity and compactness ensure a best fixed point exists. Without attainment, use an infimum. To prove regret, first compare with every fixed feasible \(u\), then select a minimizer afterward.

For \(f_1(x)=x\) and \(f_2(x)=-x\) on \([-1,1]\), every fixed point totals zero. Picking \(-1\) then \(1\) totals \(-2\), but switches comparators.

Convex differentiability gives \(f_t(u)\ge f_t(x_t)+g_t^\top(u-x_t)\). Rearranging yields the displayed upper bound. This uses the current gradient at the played point; it neither predicts tomorrow's loss nor says the gradient points toward the best fixed comparator.

In the source example \(f(x)=(x-1/2)^2\), \(x_t=-1/2\), and \(u=0\). Then \(f(x_t)=1,f(u)=1/4,g_t=-2\). The actual gap is \(3/4\); its linear upper bound is \((-2)(-1/2)=1\).

02 / 09 · Keep the update feasible

Project the proposal onto the declared set

Distinguish feasibility from the distance inequality used in the proof. PDF pp. 4–7

The main idea

For a nonempty closed convex set, Euclidean projection is the unique closest feasible point:

\[\Pi_{\mathcal K}(y)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in\mathcal K}\|x-y\|_2.\]

It restores feasibility and satisfies, for every feasible comparator \(u\),

\[\|\Pi_{\mathcal K}(y)-u\|_2^2\le\|y-u\|_2^2.\]

This compares the raw proposal with its projection, not the old decision with the new decision.

Compute box and ball projections

Closedness and distance growth give existence; strict convexity of squared distance on a convex set gives uniqueness. An already feasible proposal projects to itself. On a box, clip each coordinate to its allowed interval.

The source takes \(\mathcal K=[-1,1]^2\), \(x_t=(-0.8,0.6)\), \(g_t=(1,-2)\), and \(\eta=1/2\). The proposal is \(y=(-1.3,1.6)\); its projection is \((-1,1)\). For \(u=(0,0)\), squared distances are \(4.25\) and \(2\).

A radius-\(B\) ball instead uses a radial correction:

\[\Pi_{\{\|x\|_2\le B\}}(y)=\begin{cases}y,&\|y\|_2\le B,\\ By/\|y\|_2,&\|y\|_2>B.\end{cases}\]

Box and ball projection take work proportional to dimension. An arbitrary convex set may need a separate optimization procedure; the geometric theorem alone does not supply a cheap implementation.

Prove the comparator-distance property

Let \(p=\Pi_{\mathcal K}(y)\). For feasible \(u\), the segment \(p+s(u-p)\), \(s\in[0,1]\), stays feasible. Minimality of \(p\) gives the right derivative at zero:

\[2(p-y)^\top(u-p)\ge0,\qquad (y-p)^\top(u-p)\le0.\]

Expand using the same comparator:

\[\|y-u\|_2^2=\|y-p\|_2^2+\|p-u\|_2^2+2(y-p)^\top(p-u)\ge\|p-u\|_2^2.\]

Both the squared residual and the final inner product are nonnegative. This proves the result. A complete gradient step can still increase distance to \(u\); OGD's analysis controls accumulated changes instead.

03 / 09 · Distance as the proof quantity

Play, observe, step, project—and sum before bounding

Derive the one-round inequality and its cancellation. PDF pp. 7–10

The main idea

\[x_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal K}(x_t-\eta g_t),\qquad g_t=\nabla f_t(x_t).\]

For this proof, \(\eta>0\) is constant and \(u\) is the same fixed feasible point throughout. Projection and square expansion turn the gradient comparison into a distance difference:

\[f_t(x_t)-f_t(u)\le \frac{\|x_t-u\|_2^2-\|x_{t+1}-u\|_2^2}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta}{2}\|g_t\|_2^2.\]
Derive the one-round comparison

Projection gives \(\|x_{t+1}-u\|_2^2\le\|x_t-\eta g_t-u\|_2^2\). Expand the right side:

\[\|x_t-u\|_2^2-2\eta g_t^\top(x_t-u)+\eta^2\|g_t\|_2^2.\]

Rearrange and divide by positive \(2\eta\), then insert convex linearization. The distance difference may be negative; it need not be positive on each round. The gradient-square term is present even on a completely deterministic sequence, without a noise assumption.

Keep the cancellations visible

Write \(a_t=\|x_t-u\|_2^2\). Then \((a_1-a_2)+(a_2-a_3)+\cdots+(a_T-a_{T+1})=a_1-a_{T+1}\). The common coefficient \(1/(2\eta)\) permits cancellation. Bounding each distance difference by \(D^2\) separately would discard it.

\[\sum_t(f_t(x_t)-f_t(u))\le\frac{\|x_1-u\|_2^2-\|x_{T+1}-u\|_2^2}{2\eta}+\frac\eta2\sum_t\|g_t\|_2^2.\]

Drop the subtracted nonnegative final distance. If the diameter is at most \(D\) and every played gradient norm is at most \(G\), the result is \(D^2/(2\eta)+\eta G^2T/2\). The algorithm can be defined with varying steps, but that schedule requires a separate summation argument.

Check the source's four-round trace

On \([-1,1]\), let \(x_1=0,\eta=1/2\), and linear losses \(f_t(x)=g_tx\) with \(g=(1/2,-1,1,-1)\). OGD plays \(0,-1/4,1/4,-1/4\), with losses \(0,1/4,1/4,1/4\), total \(3/4\).

The cumulative fixed-point loss is \(-u/2\), minimized at \(u=1\) with value \(-1/2\). Regret is \(3/4-(-1/2)=5/4\). Here all proposals remain feasible, so projection changes none of them. The shared algorithm lab later reproduces this trace alongside FTL and FTRL.

04 / 09 · Turn the theorem into a usable guarantee

Supply the diameter, gradient bound, and horizon

Keep problem assumptions explicit. PDF pp. 10–11

The main idea

On a nonempty compact convex set of diameter at most \(D\), with differentiable convex losses, \(\|g_t\|_2\le G\), feasible start, and constant \(\eta>0\), OGD satisfies

\[R_T\le\frac{D^2}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta G^2T}{2}.\]

For positive \(D,G\) and known \(T\), the choice \(\eta=D/(G\sqrt T)\) balances the terms and yields \(R_T\le DG\sqrt T\).

Interpret the constants and edge cases

The guarantee holds for arbitrary realized sequences satisfying the conditions, including later losses chosen in response to past decisions. It concerns the best fixed point on that sequence.

For the four-round source trace, \(D=2,G=1,\eta=1/2\). The bound is \(4+1=5\), above the observed \(5/4\). For a separate horizon \(T=100\), the tuned step is 0.2 and the bound 20. This is a new fixed-horizon run, rather than a mid-run rate change.

When \(D=0\), there is only one feasible point. When \(G=0\) at every played point, the update stays at its start and convexity makes that point a minimizer of every current loss. Regret is zero directly; keep a positive step instead of dividing by zero. Geometry and gradient scale can depend on dimension even when the bound has no explicit \(d\).

Derive the bounds for a constrained squared-loss predictor

Let \(\|x\|_2\le B\), \(B>0\), and \(f_t(x)=(a_t^\top x-b_t)^2\). Assume the supplied feature and response bounds \(\|a_t\|_2\le A\) and \(|b_t|\le C\). Then

\[g_t=2(a_t^\top x_t-b_t)a_t,\qquad |a_t^\top x_t-b_t|\le AB+C.\]

Cauchy–Schwarz and the parameter constraint give \(\|g_t\|_2\le2(AB+C)A\). The parameter ball has diameter \(2B\). Substitute \(D=2B,G=2(AB+C)A\):

\[R_T\le4BA(AB+C)\sqrt T.\]

The comparator is one fixed parameter vector in the ball. Input and response bounds are application assumptions; OGD does not supply them automatically.

The constants describe the problem

A numerical bound requires supplied geometry and gradient control. Tuning uses them before the run.

05 / 09 · Stabilize past minimizers

Regularize the objective built from past gradients

Compare the same loss sequence under different decision rules. PDF pp. 11–14

The main idea

Follow-the-leader minimizes the sum of past original losses. Linearized FTRL instead minimizes past linearized losses plus a declared regularizer:

\[x_t=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in\mathcal K}\left[\eta\sum_{s\lt t}g_s^\top x+\mathcal R(x)\right],\qquad \mathcal R(x)=\frac\alpha2\|x\|_2^2.\]

With \(\alpha>0\), \(0\in\mathcal K\), and \(\eta>0\), the first decision is \(x_1=0\), and completing the square gives

\[x_t=\Pi_{\mathcal K}\!\left(-\frac\eta\alpha\sum_{s\lt t}g_s\right).\]
Why minimizing all past losses can switch endpoints

FTL chooses \(x_t\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{x\in\mathcal K}\sum_{s\lt t}f_s(x)\), with an initial and tie rule. It remembers the whole cumulative objective; the issue in this example is sensitivity of its minimizer.

On \([-1,1]\), use the source coefficients \(g=(1/2,-1,1,-1)\), initial FTL point 1, and tie point 1. Past sums are \(0,1/2,-1/2,1/2\). A positive sum chooses \(-1\), a negative sum chooses 1. FTL plays \(1,-1,1,-1\), incurs \(1/2,1,1,1\), and totals \(7/2\). The same best fixed point \(u=1\) loses \(-1/2\), so regret is 4.

The example shows that convexity alone does not supply a general FTL regret guarantee. It does not assert that FTL performs poorly on every sequence.

Complete the square and read the regularizer
\[\frac\alpha2\|x\|_2^2+\eta\Big(\sum_{s\lt t}g_s\Big)^\top x=\frac\alpha2\left\|x+\frac\eta\alpha\sum_{s\lt t}g_s\right\|_2^2+\text{constant}.\]

The constant is independent of \(x\). Minimizing squared distance over \(\mathcal K\) is projection, yielding the displayed update. Each \(g_s\) was evaluated at its played point and is held fixed in later objectives.

Dividing by \(\eta\) writes the objective as past linearized loss plus \(\mathcal R(x)/\eta\). A smaller rate gives the regularizer more influence. \(\mathcal R\) here is an algorithmic penalty, not population risk. For nonlinear losses, linearized FTRL differs from regularizing \(\sum_{s\lt t}f_s(x)\).

With \(\alpha=1,\eta=1/2\) on the shared source sequence, quadratic FTRL plays \(0,-1/4,1/4,-1/4\), totals \(3/4\), and has regret \(5/4\). These computed values illustrate the rule; the regret proof comes next.

06 / 09 · First FTRL connection

Use curvature to control a decision's response to one loss

Keep existence, uniqueness, and differentiability distinct. PDF pp. 14–16

The main idea

The quadratic regularizer has a precise first-order lower gap:

\[\mathcal R(y)-\mathcal R(x)-\nabla\mathcal R(x)^\top(y-x)=\frac\alpha2\|y-x\|_2^2.\]

When two cumulative objectives differ by \(\eta g_t^\top x\), this curvature controls the movement between their minimizers:

\[\|x_t-x_{t+1}\|_2\le\frac\eta\alpha\|g_t\|_2,\qquad g_t^\top(x_t-x_{t+1})\le\frac\eta\alpha\|g_t\|_2^2.\]
State strong convexity without presuming a gradient

For all \(x,y\) and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), \(\alpha\)-strong convexity in the Euclidean norm means

\[\mathcal R((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)\le(1-\lambda)\mathcal R(x)+\lambda\mathcal R(y)-\frac\alpha2\lambda(1-\lambda)\|x-y\|_2^2.\]

For differentiable \(\mathcal R\), the equivalent first-order form puts the quadratic gap below the function after its tangent. The quadratic attains that gap exactly.

The source's \(|x|+x^2/2\) is 1-strongly convex but has no ordinary derivative at zero. Strong convexity implies at most one minimizer on a convex set; existence still requires appropriate conditions. Our cumulative objectives are continuous on a compact set, so minimizers exist, and the positive quadratic curvature makes them unique. The proof below uses the differentiable quadratic.

Compare two constrained minimizers without setting their gradients to zero

Define \(H_t(x)=\mathcal R(x)+\eta\sum_{s=1}^t g_s^\top x\), with \(H_0=\mathcal R\). Then \(x_t\) minimizes \(H_{t-1}\) and \(x_{t+1}\) minimizes \(H_t\). At a constrained minimizer \(z\), the segment derivative gives \(\nabla H_t(z)^\top(v-z)\ge0\) for feasible \(v\), rather than requiring a zero gradient.

Since adding a linear term preserves curvature, \(H_t(v)\ge H_t(z)+(\alpha/2)\|v-z\|_2^2\). Apply the gap once in each direction:

\[\begin{aligned}H_{t-1}(x_{t+1})-H_{t-1}(x_t)&\ge\frac\alpha2\|x_t-x_{t+1}\|_2^2,\\H_t(x_t)-H_t(x_{t+1})&\ge\frac\alpha2\|x_t-x_{t+1}\|_2^2.\end{aligned}\]

Add them; old terms cancel because \(H_t-H_{t-1}=\eta g_t^\top x\):

\[\alpha\|x_t-x_{t+1}\|_2^2\le\eta g_t^\top(x_t-x_{t+1})\le\eta\|g_t\|_2\|x_t-x_{t+1}\|_2.\]

If movement is nonzero, divide by its norm and positive \(\alpha\). If movement is zero, the result already holds. The second displayed movement bound follows from Cauchy–Schwarz.

Constrained optimality

A boundary minimizer need not have zero gradient. Nonnegative directional derivatives into the feasible set suffice.

07 / 09 · Second FTRL connection

Pay for replacing a look-ahead point by the legal decision

Combine prefix comparison with movement. PDF pp. 16–18

The main idea

The next point \(x_{t+1}\) already uses \(g_t\), so it was unavailable when playing round \(t\). It is useful in the proof: compare those prefix minimizers with a fixed \(u\), then bound the cost of replacing them by the actually played points.

\[\sum_t g_t^\top(x_t-u)\le\frac{\mathcal R(u)-\mathcal R(x_1)}{\eta}+\sum_tg_t^\top(x_t-x_{t+1}).\]

For the declared quadratic, \(0\in\mathcal K\) gives \(x_1=0\) and \(\mathcal R(x_1)=0\).

Prove the prefix-minimizer inequality

For this induction define \(q_0(x)=\mathcal R(x)/\eta\) and \(q_t(x)=g_t^\top x\) for \(t\ge1\). The point \(x_{t+1}\) minimizes \(\sum_{s=0}^tq_s(x)\). The claim, for every feasible \(u\), is

\[\sum_{t=0}^Tq_t(x_{t+1})\le\sum_{t=0}^Tq_t(u).\]

At \(T=0\), this is the minimizing property of \(x_1\). Suppose the statement holds through \(T-1\) for every comparator. Insert \(u=x_{T+1}\), then add \(q_T(x_{T+1})\). Minimality of \(x_{T+1}\) for the complete prefix gives

\[\sum_{t=0}^Tq_t(x_{t+1})\le\sum_{t=0}^Tq_t(x_{T+1})\le\sum_{t=0}^Tq_t(u).\]

The induction uses a prefix minimizer inside a proof, not as a legal current action. Separating \(t=0\) yields \(\sum_tg_t^\top(x_{t+1}-u)\le(\mathcal R(u)-\mathcal R(x_1))/\eta\).

Insert the movement cost and obtain regret

Write \(x_t-u=(x_t-x_{t+1})+(x_{t+1}-u)\). Summing separates the movement terms from the preceding comparator term. Use convex linearization on actual loss differences and the movement bound on each linear cost. With \(\|g_t\|_2\le G\),

\[\sum_t(f_t(x_t)-f_t(u))\le\frac{\mathcal R(u)}{\eta}+\frac{\eta G^2T}{\alpha}.\]

Assume a known bound \(B_{\mathcal R}\ge\mathcal R(u)\) for every feasible \(u\). This is the quantity called \(B\) in PDF p. 18, distinguished here from the earlier parameter-ball radius. Then

\[R_T\le\frac{B_{\mathcal R}}{\eta}+\frac{\eta G^2T}{\alpha},\qquad \eta=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha B_{\mathcal R}}{G^2T}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_T\le2G\sqrt{\frac{B_{\mathcal R}T}{\alpha}}.\]

The positive-case tuning needs a known regularizer bound, rather than the unknown best comparator's eventual penalty. Since \(0\in\mathcal K\), a diameter bound \(D\) permits \(B_{\mathcal R}=\alpha D^2/2\). If \(B_{\mathcal R}=0\), the quadratic forces only the origin to be feasible. If \(G=0\), the origin remains a common minimizing point. Both give zero regret directly.

For the shared four-round example, \(B_{\mathcal R}=1/2,\alpha=G=1,\eta=1/2\), giving \(1+2=3\), above observed regret \(5/4\). At a separate horizon 100, the tuned rate is about 0.07071 and the bound about 14.142.

08 / 09 · Same order, distinct updates

Projection can separate the decisions

Identify when a cumulative sum and an incremental step agree. PDF p. 19

The main idea

Without a constraint, quadratic FTRL started at zero obeys \(x_t=-(\eta/\alpha)\sum_{s\lt t}g_s\), so subtraction gives the gradient-descent recursion with effective step \(\eta/\alpha\). Projection generally breaks that identity.

\[x_{t+1}^{\mathrm{OGD}}=\Pi_{\mathcal K}(x_t^{\mathrm{OGD}}-\eta g_t),\qquad x_{t+1}^{\mathrm{FTRL}}=\Pi_{\mathcal K}\!\left(-\frac\eta\alpha\sum_{s\le t}g_s\right).\]
Reproduce the source's active-constraint example

Use \(\mathcal K=[-1,1]\), initial zero, \(\eta=\alpha=1\), and gradients \((2,-1)\). Both first proposals are \(-2\) and both give \(x_2=-1\).

After the next gradient, OGD steps from its already projected point: \(x_3^{\mathrm{OGD}}=\Pi(-1-(-1))=0\). FTRL projects the scaled complete sum: \(x_3^{\mathrm{FTRL}}=\Pi(-(2-1))=-1\). Both are feasible and use the same gradients.

Select the second preset in the algorithm lab to inspect the two decisions. The unconstrained algebra alone does not establish a bounded-domain regret theorem on all of \(\mathbb R^d\). Equal square-root orders under their respective assumptions do not make two update operations identical.

09 / 09 · Match the guarantee to the question

Keep worst-case rates, observed traces, and future loss distinct

Carry the conditions into the conclusion. PDF pp. 20–22

The main idea

OGD controls accumulated distance changes; quadratic FTRL controls cumulative minimizers and movement. Their balanced general-convex-loss bounds have square-root order for fixed geometry and gradient bounds. An observed finite trace, a worst-case order statement, and a stochastic population guarantee are different claims.

What does a matching worst-case order say?

The source states that on a nontrivial interval of diameter \(D>0\), with convex losses of gradient magnitude at most \(G>0\), any online algorithm can face a sequence with expected regret at least \(cDG\sqrt T\) for a universal constant \(c>0\). Expectation permits randomized algorithms and a randomized difficult sequence. The sheet states this result without supplying a full lower-bound proof or a numerical value of \(c\).

It concerns the worst case over the class, not a lower bound for every sequence. Linear losses belong to that class. A strongly convex regularizer does not remove linear losses or make the losses themselves strongly convex, so regularizer curvature alone does not establish general \(O(\log T)\) regret here.

When can an average decision describe population performance?

Add the source's stochastic assumptions: each current loss is a fresh independent draw from one distribution, decisions use only the past, and \(F(x)=\mathbb E[f_t(x)]\) is finite. Keep the convex compact domain and bounded-gradient conditions. With appropriate OGD steps, output the average of played points:

\[\bar x_T=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T x_t\in\mathcal K.\]

Convexity keeps the average feasible. Independence relates a played point's expected fresh loss to \(F\); convexity supports averaging. The qualified conclusion is expected excess population objective of order \(DG/\sqrt T\):

\[\mathbb E[F(\bar x_T)]-\min_{u\in\mathcal K}F(u)=O(DG/\sqrt T).\]

This concerns the averaged decision under extra sampling assumptions, rather than every last iterate or arbitrary future data. Dividing adversarial regret by \(T\) alone does not establish those assumptions.

1. Identify where curvature is assumed

A method has a strongly convex regularizer and general convex losses. Does this fact alone establish logarithmic regret?

Show solution

No. Curvature of the penalty does not strengthen curvature of the losses. The source's general-loss guarantee has square-root order.

2. Name the output and the added assumptions

What does the stochastic statement concern, and what is added beyond adversarial sequence regret?

Show solution

It concerns expected excess \(F\) at the average \(\bar x_T\). It adds fresh independent loss draws from one distribution, past-only decisions, finite population objective, and the stated convexity, geometry, and gradient conditions. It is not an unconditional last-iterate guarantee.
